TEST: B. Relativistic time dilation derivation
PROMPT: Why does time dilate at relativistic speeds? Keep it concise but accurate.
==================================================

--- MODEL: groq/compound-mini ---
Time dilation follows directly from the two postulates of special relativity:

1. **The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames.**  
2. **The speed of light in vacuum, \(c\), is the same for all observers, regardless of their relative motion.**

From these postulates the Lorentz transformation relates the coordinates \((t,x)\) in one inertial frame \(S\) to \((t',x')\) in a frame \(S'\) moving at speed \(v\) along the \(x\)‑axis:

\[
\begin{aligned}
t' &= \gamma\!\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^{2}}\right),\\[4pt]
x' &= \gamma\!\left(x - vt\right),\qquad 
\gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}} .
\end{aligned}
\]

Consider a clock at rest in \(S'\) (so \(x'=\text{const}\)). Its world‑line satisfies \(dx'=0\), giving

\[
0 = \gamma\!\left(dx - v\,dt\right) \;\Rightarrow\; dx = v\,dt .
\]

Substituting \(dx = v\,dt\) into the time transformation:

\[
dt' = \gamma\!\left(dt - \frac{v\,dx}{c^{2}}\right)
     = \gamma\!\left(dt - \frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}dt\right)
     = \frac{dt}{\gamma}.
\]

Thus the interval between two ticks measured in the moving frame \(S\) is

\[
\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t' .
\]

Since \(\gamma > 1\) for any non‑zero \(v\), the moving clock (\(\Delta t'\)) records **less elapsed time** than the stationary observer measures (\(\Delta t\)). In other words, **time runs slower in a frame that moves at relativistic speed relative to an observer**.

The effect is a geometric consequence of preserving the invariant spacetime interval \(c^{2}t^{2}-x^{2}\) across inertial frames, which is required by the constancy of \(c\). Hence, time dilation is not a dynamical slowing of processes but a manifestation of how space and time mix under Lorentz transformations.

Time dilation follows directly from the Lorentz transformation, which preserves the invariant spacetime interval  

\[
\Delta s^{2}=c^{2}\Delta t^{2}-\Delta x^{2}=c^{2}\Delta\tau^{2},
\]

where \(\Delta\tau\) is the **proper time** measured by a clock that is at rest in its own frame (so \(\Delta x=0\)).  

For an observer in a different inertial frame in which the clock moves with speed \(v\) along the \(x\)‑axis, \(\Delta x = v\,\Delta t\). Substituting gives  

\[
c^{2}\Delta t^{2}-v^{2}\Delta t^{2}=c^{2}\Delta\tau^{2}
\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;
\Delta t = \frac{\Delta\tau}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}
           \equiv \gamma\,\Delta\tau ,
\]

with \(\displaystyle \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}/c^{2}}}\ge 1\).

Thus the coordinate time interval \(\Delta t\) between two ticks of the moving clock, as measured by the stationary observer, is larger than the proper interval \(\Delta\tau\). In other words, **moving clocks run slower** by the factor \(\gamma\).

The effect is geometric, not dynamical: it arises because different inertial frames slice spacetime into “space” and “time” differently, while the spacetime interval remains unchanged. Consequently, at speeds approaching \(c\) (\(v\to c\)), \(\gamma\) grows without bound and the observed ticking rate approaches zero. This is the relativistic time‑dilation formula.

### The physics of a space‑elevator tether

For a tether that extends from the planetary surface to the synchronous orbit, the *minimum* specific strength a material must have is roughly

\[
\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\;>\; g_0\,L ,
\]

where  

* \(g_0\) is the surface gravity,  
* \(L\) is the tether length, and  
* \(\sigma/\rho\) is the material’s specific tensile strength (strength divided by density).

The derivation follows from the differential tension balance  

\[
\frac{dT}{dr}= \rho A(r) g_{\text{eff}}(r),
\]

and the requirement that the tension never exceed the material’s ultimate stress \(\sigma\). Integrating from the surface to the top gives the exponential taper factor, and the inequality above is the condition for a *finite* taper.

\[
